# Lore Bible — Thistledown Academy: The Hollow Bell

This document is the single source of truth for setting, characters, and plot. Any dialogue,
item, or location text generated for this project should stay consistent with what's written
here. If something here needs to change, edit it here first, then propagate the change to
content — don't let content drift from the bible.

**Guardrail:** everything below is original. The "deadpan outcast + cheerful roommate"
pairing is a common trope, not owned by anyone — but names, backstories, the exact nature of
the Omen Glass, and the exact plot beats of the Hollow Bell must stay unique to this project.
Don't reuse specific names, specific ability mechanics, or specific plot beats from any
existing show.

---

## 1. Setting

**Thistledown Academy** is a boarding school for "unusual" students — quiet oddities, minor
uncanny talents, kids who don't fit anywhere else — set at the edge of a fog-bound town with a
buried history. The school is old, drafty, and administratively indifferent to anything it
can't put on a brochure.

- **Town:** unnamed in dialogue by default, referred to as "town" or "downhill." Founded
  around the same time as the school, a century ago. Local economy is thin — a general store,
  a diner, a boarded-up mill by the lake.
- **The Lake:** Blackwater Lake. Cold, deep, ringed by dead reeds. Where most disappearances
  cluster. Locals avoid the north shore after dark without ever quite explaining why.
- **The Bell Tower:** disused, structurally "condemned" per the administration, locked. Once
  rang the hour for the whole school; hasn't rung on schedule in decades. When it rings off
  schedule, that's the Hollow Bell — and someone goes missing shortly after.
- **Time of year:** autumn into early winter over the course of the game. Muted colors, early
  dusk, fog.

## 2. The Legend of the Hollow Bell

A century ago, the school's founders made some kind of bargain to keep the lake "quiet" —
details deliberately vague to students, half-erased in the official record, and the actual
truth is worse and more specific than the campfire version. The bell in the tower was cast as
part of that bargain. Nobody currently at the school remembers why. The administration's
official position is that "the Hollow Bell" is a folk story townsfolk use to explain lake
drownings, and repeating it is grounds for a talking-to.

**Ground truth (for the writer, not spelled out to the player early):** Groundskeeper Aldous
Crane maintains a binding in the bell tower that keeps something contained — something the
founders trapped rather than destroyed. The binding is failing and needs periodic
"feeding" — hence the disappearances. Crane isn't a monster for spectacle; he inherited the job
(literally or figuratively — keep this ambiguous until late-game) and believes the alternative
to what he's doing is worse. The player's job is to figure out what he's actually doing, why,
and decide how the binding — and Crane — get resolved.

## 3. Player Character

- Customizable name and appearance.
- Voice: dry, deadpan, minimal-affect. Not cold — observant, a little tired, funny in a
  flat way. Reacts to escalating weirdness with the same tone as reacting to bad cafeteria
  food. This voice is a defining design constraint for every player dialogue line.
- **The Omen Glass:** a cracked compact mirror, a family heirloom. Once per in-game day at
  dusk, the player can ask it one question (from a short list tied to current progress). It
  shows a fragmented, cryptic image or phrase — never a direct answer, always a nudge. This is
  the built-in hint system, framed in-world as a ritual, not a raw "psychic vision" power. The
  Glass doesn't explain itself and the player character doesn't fully trust it either.

## 4. Supporting Cast

### Junie Okafor — roommate
Aggressively, unkillably cheerful. Runs the school's photography club (mostly an excuse to
wander places she isn't supposed to be, which is narratively convenient). Becomes a reluctant
investigation partner — reluctant because she's scared, not because she doesn't care. Comic
foil to the player's deadpan: she narrates everything out loud, the player does not. Her
photography gives her a legitimate in-fiction reason to have pictures of places/times other
characters weren't supposed to be.

### Priya Voss — popular student
Dismissive of the player early on — not cruel, just socially uninterested. Has a personal
stake in the disappearances that surfaces mid-game (a sibling, close friend, or someone she
was supposed to meet the night they vanished — pick one and commit during Phase 3 writing).
Once her stake surfaces, she becomes a possible ally with resources the player doesn't have
(access to staff, campus history, social leverage).

### Ms. Halloway — faculty member
Deliberately ambiguous through most of the branching investigation: could be a genuine ally
who knows more than she's allowed to say, a red herring the player wastes time on, or
complicit with Crane out of the same inherited-obligation logic. Which one she is can depend on
which clues/connections the player makes first — keep her dialogue non-committal enough in
early branches to support more than one resolution.

### Groundskeeper Aldous Crane — antagonist
Outwardly harmless, background scenery to staff and students — the guy raking leaves nobody
looks at twice. Performs bindings in the bell tower to sustain what's trapped there. His
reasons are revealed gradually through the investigation, not stated up front. Not a
mustache-twirler: play him as tired and burdened, someone doing a job he didn't choose and
can't figure out how to stop doing.

## 5. Tone

Dry humor, understatement, gothic-autumn aesthetic. This is "quietly unsettling small town
with a secret," not jump-scare horror. Comedy comes from the contrast between the player's
flat affect and Junie's enthusiasm, and from the administration's bureaucratic denial of
increasingly obvious supernatural activity. Dread comes from pacing and implication, not gore.

## 6. Naming & Style Conventions

- Keep place names plain and slightly wry (Blackwater Lake, the Archive, the greenhouse) —
  avoid overly ornate fantasy naming.
- Clue and item text should read like real documents (yearbook captions, newspaper clippings,
  logbook entries), not exposition dumps.
- Player dialogue options should default to short, flat sentences. Avoid exclamation points
  in player lines even when other characters use them.
